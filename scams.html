Scam guide

Spot the scam before the money moves

These are the scams I see most as a fraud analyst at a credit union. Each one comes with the warning signs to watch for and what to do. Share it with anyone who might need it.

Matthew Suggs · Fraud Analyst

The one rule to remember

Your credit union will never call, text or email you to ask for a one-time code or your password, or to tell you to move money to keep it safe.

If anyone asks, hang up and call the number on the back of your card.

Common scams

Account takeover

A scammer gets into your online banking, usually by tricking you into sharing a password or a one-time code. They change your contact details so alerts go to them, then move money out.

Warning signs

  • A code or password reset you didn't ask for
  • An alert that your email, phone number or address changed
  • A login from a device or place you don't recognize
  • Someone asking you to read back a code to verify your identity

What to do

  • Never share a one-time code with anyone, even someone who says they're from your credit union
  • Turn on account alerts and two-step verification
  • Call your credit union right away if you see a change you didn't make

Impersonation calls and texts

Someone pretends to be your credit union's fraud department, the police, a government agency or tech support. Caller ID can be faked, so the call may look real. They create panic, then ask for codes, gift cards, or a transfer to a new account to keep your money safe.

Warning signs

  • Pressure to act right now, or a threat of arrest or a frozen account
  • Being told to keep it secret, even from credit union staff
  • Requests for gift cards, cryptocurrency, a wire or a payment app transfer
  • Caller ID that shows your credit union's real number

What to do

  • Hang up and call the number on the back of your card or on the official website
  • Never move money because a caller told you to
  • Tell credit union staff the whole story, especially if someone coached you on what to say

Fake and stolen checks

You get a check for more than you're owed, from a buyer, a new job, a prize or a mystery shopper offer, and you're asked to send back the difference. Days later the check bounces and you owe the full amount. Checks stolen from the mail can also be washed and rewritten.

Warning signs

  • A check for more than the agreed amount
  • A request to send part of it back by wire, gift card or payment app
  • A job or prize you didn't apply for that pays up front

What to do

  • Remember that money showing as available doesn't mean the check has cleared
  • Never send money back from a check you just deposited
  • Mail checks inside the post office, not from your home mailbox

Wire and payment app scams

Wires and payment app transfers move money fast and are very hard to get back, so scammers push for them. Common versions are an online romance that turns into money trouble, an investment with guaranteed returns, and fake wiring instructions right before a home closing.

Warning signs

  • Someone you've only met online needs money
  • Guaranteed returns, or pressure to invest in crypto
  • Wiring instructions that change by email at the last minute

What to do

  • Confirm wiring instructions by calling a number you already trust
  • Never send money to someone you haven't met in person
  • Talk it over with someone you trust before sending a large amount

If it already happened

  1. Call your credit union or bank right away, using the number on your card. The sooner they know, the better the chance of stopping the money.
  2. Change your passwords for online banking and for the email account tied to it.
  3. Report it to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov. If your identity was stolen, use IdentityTheft.gov to get a recovery plan.
  4. Report internet scams to the FBI at ic3.gov.
  5. Consider freezing your credit at Equifax, Experian and TransUnion. It's free, and it stops anyone opening new credit in your name.

This guide is general education. It doesn't describe real cases and isn't advice from my employer. If you think you've been scammed, contact your financial institution first.
